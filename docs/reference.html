The financial-planning knowledge graph: value, use, and validation

Why the firm encodes task ordering as a governed knowledge graph, how customer facts traverse it without becoming graph data, and how each graph version is checked before it can influence a recommendation.

Why this exists

The knowledge graph is a directed planning workflow: task nodes such as emergency_fund, pay_down_high_interest, and employer_match_401k encode the firm's intended order of financial work. HAS_CHILD relationships encode progression. Where progression depends on a customer fact, a CRITERIA_BRANCH relationship carries a condition_key/condition_value and points to a CriteriaNode; that node's HAS_CHILD relationship points to the task selected by the branch.

The graph stores planning knowledge, not customer records. At request time, an application supplies the customer facts needed by the relevant branches and uses them as traversal context. The scenario profiles passed to walk() in this repository model that separation: the facts are inputs to a traversal and are never written into the graph or its snapshot.

Because that output reaches real customers, a bad edge, a missing precondition, or an accidental new route through the graph is a compliance and trust problem, not just a bug. validate.py exists to catch that before a graph change ships, not after a client has acted on it.

What the firm is investing in

The database footprint is deliberately bounded: Neo4j holds the planning knowledge graph—tasks, ordering, branch criteria, rationales, and source-document references. Customer data remains in its systems of record and is supplied only when an application traverses the graph. The stored graph therefore grows with the planning model, not linearly with the customer base. Query volume still grows with use, but Neo4j does not become a second customer-data platform.

Institutional planning knowledge
The firm's view of what work should precede what is explicit, inspectable data rather than ordering hidden across application code and operational playbooks.
One governed source for multiple products
Eligibility filtering and next-step guidance can use the same tasks, criteria, and ordering instead of maintaining two rule sets that can drift apart.
Explainability and change control
A result can be traced to a task, the branch that selected it, and the task's rationale and source-document reference. A graph snapshot makes structural changes reviewable and reproducible.
Impact analysis
Because ordering is explicit, the firm can enumerate structural routes, compare graph versions, and test whether a change introduces an unapproved route or breaks an invariant.

The meaningful investment is therefore not large-scale customer storage. It is the curation and governance of the firm's planning model: agreeing on task definitions, ordering, eligibility criteria, rationale, ownership, and review. That is also where most of the durable value sits.

Application today: next-best messages

Today the firm uses the knowledge graph to decide which planning message or task should be presented next. Bulk eligibility filtering and next-step traversal are two internal query patterns that power this one application; they are not separate customer or advisor products.

Future applications the KG can support

The planning graph can become a shared decision layer for customer experiences, advisor tools, operations, and governance. These are distinct applications the firm could build; none should be read as already implemented.

Customer and advisor applications

Personalized financial roadmap
Experience: a customer sees completed, current, and upcoming planning tasks as a coherent roadmap rather than isolated messages. KG use: traverse forward from the current task to applicable goals or terminal tasks and explain the order of the remaining plan.
Advisor planning copilot
Experience: an advisor sees the recommended next action, viable alternatives, unmet prerequisites, and the policy rationale in one workspace. KG use: combine eligible-task filtering with forward and reverse traversal, then attach each task's rationale and source-document reference.
Goal and life-event planner
Experience: a customer or advisor selects a goal—buying a home, retiring, starting a family, changing jobs—and sees the work that goal introduces or reprioritizes. KG use: connect goals and life events to affected tasks, then traverse prerequisites and applicable routes using supplied customer facts.
Progressive onboarding and data collection
Experience: the application asks only for information needed to make the next planning decision instead of presenting a long generic questionnaire. KG use: inspect unmatched branches and missing condition_key values to choose the next useful question.
Proactive planning alerts
Experience: when an external customer fact changes, the firm can identify newly relevant tasks and alert the customer or advisor. KG use: re-run eligibility and traversal with the new facts; customer records remain in their source systems.

Operating and governance applications

Planning case and workflow orchestration
Application: turn an approved plan into sequenced work, assign the next task, and assemble required documents and handoffs. KG use: extend tasks with owner, document, workflow, and dependency relationships, then continue traversal beyond recommendation selection.
Compliance and policy change-impact workbench
Application: show reviewers which tasks, messages, customer routes, and downstream policies could change before a graph update is approved. KG use: traverse upstream dependencies and downstream descendants, then compare affected paths across graph versions.
Recommendation explanation and audit service
Application: answer why a recommendation was made and reproduce the decision later. KG use: retain the selected path, matched criteria, graph version, and external fact references; replay them against the corresponding snapshot without storing the customer record in Neo4j.
Planning simulation and coverage lab
Application: planners test new policy or task designs against synthetic customer situations before release. KG use: traverse scenario profiles through proposed graph versions and surface dead ends, ambiguous branches, unreachable goals, and changed routes.
Portfolio opportunity and service intelligence
Application: identify common planning gaps, bottlenecks, and newly eligible services across the customer base. KG use: aggregate traversal outcomes outside Neo4j by stable task and criterion identifiers, then map those aggregate results back onto the planning graph.

Each application reuses the same governed task definitions, ordering, criteria, rationale, and source references. The economic leverage comes from building multiple experiences on one planning model while customer data stays in the systems designed to hold it.

Why a graph alongside rules

The decisions here—is a task complete, is the 401(k) match maxed, does a profile satisfy a condition—are genuinely rules. The criteria node does not evaluate a customer. At traversal time, application logic compares a supplied fact with the condition_key/condition_value on a CRITERIA_BRANCH. A rules engine such as Drools could produce the same normalized fact or make the same decision.

As a Drools-style rule 
when
    Client(employerMatchPct < capPct)
then
    recommend(increase_401k_contribution);
As a graph criteria node 
employer_match_401k -CRITERIA_BRANCH-> (CriteriaNode)
  condition_key: employer_match_status
  condition_value: not_maxed
(CriteriaNode) -HAS_CHILD-> increase_401k_contribution

The graph example assumes the raw percentage has already been normalized to employer_match_status. The decision can live in either place; the graph's role is to make the resulting ordering and reachability explicit.

A rules engine can implement the individual decisions. The graph earns its place because it represents how those decisions compose: what can follow a task, which tasks are reachable, and which structural routes a graph version contains. In a rules-only design, that ordering is often implicit in rule-firing dependencies or duplicated in another model, so reachability and change-impact questions require simulation, static analysis, or a separately maintained dependency map. They are not impossible; they are simply less direct than traversing explicit relationships.

employer_match_401k
→ roth_ira → estate_review
→ roth_ira → taxable_brokerage
→ taxable_brokerage

One task, three structurally permitted continuations in the sample approved_paths. A customer's actual continuation depends on which branch conditions match the facts supplied at each fork.

That is the graph's technical value in this repository: enumerate_paths lists every simple structural route up to the 5,000-path safety cap. That set is diffable across graph versions and checkable against invariants with check_rules, because ordering is data rather than a byproduct of firing order. Enumeration alone does not prove that every route's branch conditions are jointly satisfiable; named scenario checks provide separate evidence for specific customer profiles.

Rules and graph therefore compose. Rules determine eligibility from customer facts; the graph governs ordering and reachability; validation checks the resulting planning model. The graph database does not make the financial decisions correct by itself—the value comes from making the firm's decisions explicit, reusable, and governable.

Setup & usage

uv sync, then, without Neo4j, using a generated sample graph:

cd src
uv run python make_sample_graph.py
uv run python validate.py snapshots/sample/graph.graphml expectations.yaml

Against a real Neo4j instance:

cd src
NEO4J_URI=bolt://localhost:7687 NEO4J_USER=neo4j NEO4J_PASSWORD=... uv run python snapshot.py
uv run python validate.py snapshots/<timestamp>/graph.graphml expectations.yaml

validate.py writes paths.json and validation.json into the snapshot directory and exits 1 on failure, so it is suitable as a CI gate. The workflow in this repository validates the generated sample graph; gating a live Neo4j graph requires the same command to run against a real snapshot in the firm's deployment process.

expectations.yaml

The file every validation run checks the graph against. Kept in version control next to the code, so a graph change that breaks an expected recommendation fails the build instead of reaching a client.

expected_entries / expected_terminals
The exact allowed entry points, and the allowed set of dead ends. A new one showing up means a route changed without anyone intending it to.
approved_paths
Every simple structural route the graph is allowed to contain. Regenerated from paths.json when a change is intentional—the diff on this list is the record of which routes a change added or removed.
rules
Precedence and mutual-exclusion invariants checked against the structural routes enumerated in each validation run.
scenarios
Concrete, supplied customer profiles with the task sequence they must produce, for spot-checking conditional behavior the structural checks cannot see.

The validators

validate.py runs four complementary layers. Each catches a different class of problem; path and rule checks both depend on successful structural path enumeration.

1

Structural

check_structure()

Is the graph shaped the way you think it is?

  • acyclic — a recommendation flow can never loop
  • entry points match expected_entries — nothing silently added or removed
  • every node reachable from an entry point — no orphaned node
  • every dead end is a declared terminal, not an accident
  • every task node carries rationale + source_doc
  • every CRITERIA_BRANCH edge carries condition_key + condition_value

Cheap, exhaustive. Says nothing about whether the graph's behavior is correct — only its shape and metadata.

2

Path enumeration

enumerate_paths() / check_paths()

Every simple entry-to-terminal route in the graph topology, diffed against approved_paths. Flags any structural route nobody approved, and any approved route that's no longer present.

Exhaustive and exact for graph topology when it completes. It does not test whether all conditions along every route can be satisfied by one customer profile. Past MAX_PATHS (5,000), it refuses to enumerate rather than hang or silently truncate.

3

Rules

check_rules()

Every enumerated structural route is checked against approved invariants. A precedence rule requires two tasks to appear in the specified order when both are present; a mutual-exclusion rule prohibits the named tasks from appearing together.

A retained rule is automatically applied to newly enumerated routes in future validation runs. It does not evaluate routes that were not enumerated in the current run. Candidates are bootstrapped from the current path list by mine_rules.py and reviewed before being added to expectations.yaml—see Signing off.

4

Scenario

check_scenarios()

Does a handful of named, concrete customer profiles, supplied as traversal inputs, produce the expected task sequence?

Not exhaustive. Exists because a graph can pass layers 1–3 — correctly shaped, only approved paths, no invariant broken — and still route an actual real-world profile down the wrong one of those approved paths.

Signing off on a graph with combinatorial paths

For whoever is asked to approve a graph or a graph change — typically a compliance or risk reviewer outside the engineering team.

The instinctive request — "review every possible client journey" — is not something a person can do past a few dozen paths, and it gets worse fast: every additional sequential fork roughly doubles the journey count.

At real graph sizes—hundreds of nodes and edges—the number of distinct structural routes can reach the thousands. MAX_PATHS bounds the computation: beyond 5,000 routes the script fails loudly instead of hanging or silently truncating. A graph above that cap needs a different validation strategy before this pipeline can approve it.

Which means "read every path and sign off on each one" cannot be the review process — asking for it produces the opposite of oversight, because nobody actually reads 5,000 sequences, and the sign-off becomes a formality instead of a real check. The review has to be scoped down to something a person can hold in their head and genuinely mean their approval of. Three tiers do that:

  1. 1

    You approve rules, not paths.

    The thing worth a reviewer's judgment is not "is journey #4,812 acceptable" but the invariant behind it—when emergency-fund and investing tasks both appear, the emergency-fund task comes first; debt payoff and credit-limit-increase suggestions must never appear on the same journey; when a tax-bracket check and tax-advantaged withdrawal both appear, the check comes first. These suitability-style statements are the kind of judgment a compliance reviewer already makes, and a curated rule set is typically much smaller than the route set it governs.

  2. 2

    The rules are machine-checkable on every graph change.

    Once a rule is written down, check_rules can apply it consistently to each successfully enumerated graph version. The current CI workflow proves this process on the sample graph; the same validation must be wired into the live graph's change process before claiming it as a production gate.

  3. 3

    You only see raw paths when something is new and high-risk.

    paths.json and approved_paths make it possible to identify which structural routes are new and which approved routes vanished. A review process can then scope attention to routes touching high-risk tasks such as money movement, tax elections, or debt restructuring. The current scripts emit the full route set; risk-based filtering is a review step, not an implemented validator feature.

How a rule gets written

Rules aren't invented from nothing — they're read directly off the paths that are already approved:

  1. Start from approved_paths. Look at the journeys already signed off on and ask what invariant explains why those exist and no others do.
  2. State it in plain language. A sentence a compliance reviewer would recognize as an ordinary suitability rule, not a graph-theory statement.
  3. Write it as one of the two shapes check_rules() understands — precedence (a always before b) or mutual_exclusion (a and b never together) — the same small vocabulary mine_rules.py mines automatically.
  4. The reviewer approves the sentence and the rule together — not by re-deriving it, but by confirming the rule actually says what the sentence claims.
  5. Run it on every future graph change. Once validation is part of the live graph's change process, the retained rule covers newly enumerated routes, not just those that existed on the day of review.

That last step is why retained rules complement path review. A path-by-path review only certifies the routes that exist on the day of review; a retained rule can be re-applied to newly enumerated routes after a future fork is added. That protection depends on keeping the rule and running validation as part of the graph's change process.

mine_rules.py automates step one. Point it at a verified graph's paths.json and it mines every precedence and mutual-exclusion invariant that already holds — a drafted rule set to review, instead of a blank page:

uv run python mine_rules.py snapshots/sample/paths.json
# wrote 9 candidate rules to snapshots/sample/mined_rules.yaml

Review mined_rules.yaml, keep the ones actually meant to hold, and paste them into expectations.yaml's rules: list. From then on check_rules() enforces them on every run — the third validator layer above.

Two rule types, mined from this graph's paths

Precedence

"When a route contains both the 401(k)-match and Roth IRA tasks, the match task comes first."

- type: precedence
  before: employer_match_401k
  after: roth_ira

One of 9 precedence rules mine_rules.py found by comparing every pair of tasks across the current approved_paths. This ordering held in every current route containing both tasks, with no rule hand-written first.

This rule does not require employer_match_401k to be present whenever roth_ira appears. A stronger “B requires A” policy needs another validator rule type; check_rules() does not implement that semantics today.

Mutual exclusion

"Debt payoff and a credit-limit-increase suggestion must never appear on the same journey."

- type: mutual_exclusion
  steps: [pay_down_high_interest, credit_limit_increase]

credit_limit_increase isn't a step in this sample graph yet, so the miner can't find this one — a rule like this still has to be hand-written before the step exists, which is exactly the case a miner alone can't cover.

Neither of these needed a reviewer to look at a single row of paths.json — the first was found automatically; the second still took a person noticing a gap the graph has no data for yet. That's the actual division of labor: mine_rules.py drafts what's already true, a reviewer adds what should be true before it's ever tested by a real path.

What sign-off should mean, concretely:

  • the rule set, as written, is correct and complete for the risk you're accountable for
  • the new or high-risk journeys surfaced in this change's diff are acceptable

A reviewer is not attesting to having read every one of the graph's thousands of possible journeys — nobody can, and a process that implies otherwise isn't oversight, it's a rubber stamp with extra steps.

Every task node carries non-empty rationale and source_doc fields, enforced by layer 1. Treat them as audit pointers: the validator confirms that the fields exist, but it does not verify that the referenced document exists or supports the rationale.
